My Hackathon Journey

Two hackathons, two very different problems: tracking space debris in real time with NASA Space Apps Sarawak, and helping teachers save time with AI at HackWknd Kota Samarahan. Here's what got built, and how it placed.

Why hackathons

I've taken part in two hackathons so far: NASA Space Apps Challenge Sarawak (2021) and HackWknd Kota Samarahan (2024). Both gave a small team 48–72 hours to turn an unfamiliar problem statement into a working prototype. This page walks through what each event asked for, what we built, and how it placed — including a short walkthrough of the LearnLog prototype from HackWknd.

NASA Space Apps Sarawak 2021 — DebMap

Team
ROCKSPACE
Challenge
Mapping Space Trash in Real Time
Tech stack
JavaScript, Cesium.js, Satellite.js
Outcome
2nd Runner-Up (Sarawak, Senior) · Global Finalist & Honourable Mention

DebMap plots space debris on a live 3D globe — pulling TLE (Two-Line Element) orbital data from Celestrak, predicting each object's path and speed with Satellite.js, and flagging anything within 100km of Earth as high-risk. Built over the Space Apps weekend with teammates Muhammad Kamal and Haidil, it placed 2nd Runner-Up in the Sarawak Senior Category and went on to be recognised as a Global Finalist and Honourable Mention at the international level.

HackWknd Kota Samarahan 2024 — LearnLog

Team
Visionaries 2.0
Theme
AI for Education · UNIMAS, 6–8 Dec 2024
Tech / prototype
Figma prototype, AI question-generation pipeline
Outcome
3rd Place — Technical Excellent Award (RM1,000)

LearnLog cuts down a teacher's repetitive prep work: upload a syllabus and it generates curriculum-aligned exam questions, and separately detects which syllabus chapter a given question belongs to. It won the Technical Excellent Award (3rd place) among AI-for-Education submissions from across Sarawak.

Team presentation

Team ROCKSPACE's official presentation for NASA Space Apps Sarawak 2021 — the problem statement, the DebMap solution, and the result.

Read video transcript

Hi everyone, I'm Wan Muhadzdzib from team ROCKSPACE. ROCKSPACE consists of three team members — here with me are Kamal and Haidil.

The challenge we chose is "Mapping Space Trash in Real Time," and the reason is we liked the challenging yet interesting characteristics of this challenge. Despite the difficulties in solving it, we were able to gain new knowledge, improve our communication and problem-solving skills, and — most importantly — gain new experience, since this was our first time joining a hackathon.

Did you know that there are over 128 million pieces of space debris floating above us? And did you know this number is increasing over time, as collisions between space debris create even more debris? This collisional cascading phenomenon is known as Kessler syndrome. The increasing amount of space debris above us not only limits our access to space, but also endangers current and future space exploration missions.

To tackle this challenge, we came up with a web application called DebMap.

As you can see here, I'm demonstrating the prototype of DebMap that we created using Figma. This prototype is the closest picture of what the DebMap web application's user interface should look like.

DebMap has the ability to visualize space debris on a virtual globe and classify the space debris based on altitude data in real time. In addition, DebMap can also predict the orbital track of the debris.

So how does any of this work? We are using Two-Line Element set data — TLE data for short — obtained from Celestrak. This data is the foundation of DebMap, and it contains all the information required for the visualization, classification and prediction of debris.

First, we gather all the debris information and TLE data from the Celestrak catalog. The data is then stored into a JSON file with proper formatting for accessibility purposes. From this JSON file, we use the Cesium.js library to read the data and visualize it on a virtual globe — Cesium.js is a JavaScript library that provides a virtual globe and TLE data visualization.

TLE data also provides altitude for the debris, so we can classify the debris and colour-code it based on the safe-Earth distance, which is 100 kilometres. For prediction, we use the Satellite.js library, which uses the SGP4/SDP4 model to calculate the orbital track prediction.

Concerning the increasing amount of space debris above us, we hope that this application can provide the correct location of the space debris and classify all of the space debris using the data provided in the Celestrak catalog. We also hope this application can benefit users before they begin their space exploration, and that they can use DebMap to navigate their spacecraft while avoiding debris.

We also hope this application will benefit society by helping curb incidents where space trash crashes in a populated area, because that would be catastrophic.

With that, that's all from me. Thank you, and see ya — bye!
